room — the set
==============

USE WHEN
  Establishing where we are, and any beat where the host is on screen. The
  room is the bottom layer of every composited shot.

MEMBERS (9 angles x 2 aspects)
  wide                     the establishing shot. Opens a section.
  wide-tight               same set, closer. The far wall stops being drawn —
                           use it when a headline band needs the top third.
  desk-front               straight on at the desk. The workhorse for
                           talking-to-camera beats.
  desk-corner              three-quarter on the desk. Has a caption slot; use
                           it when an object in the set needs naming.
  from-behind-the-monitor  over the screen backs, toward him. For the beat
                           where he is reading, not addressing us.
  whiteboard-wall          for a structure plate. The wall gives the diagram
                           a reason to exist in the room.
  printer-corner           for paper — a filing, a transcript, a printout.
  doorway                  arrival and exit. Pairs with
                           host/walking-out-of-frame.
  wall-of-calls            the receipts wall. Its own slot set (below); this
                           is a content plate that happens to be a room.

SURFACE
  Rooms are drawn on the plain NIGHT CARD, never on the legal pad. A surface
  goes under the drawing; the pad's blue rules and red margin are furniture of
  the page, and on a room plate they run straight through the desk, the props
  and the host. A rule crossing a man's chest is not a surface he is standing
  on. The binding lives in engine/build.js (NOTE_SURFACES) so a caller cannot
  choose wrongly — ruled paper is for things that ARE notes.

SHADOW
  A dark reads as shadow, not as an object, when three things are true: it is
  densest where it meets whatever casts it and lightens away from it, its edge
  is ragged rather than a drawn outline, and the ground shows through it. A
  shadow is the surface in shade — not a new object sitting on top of one. The
  first version of the under-desk mass and the foreground crop had none of the
  three, and as flat slabs of uniform hatch they became the biggest darks in
  frame and pulled attention off the host.

VALUE RANGE
  Three or four genuinely dark OBJECTS carry the frame: the shadow under the
  desk, the back of the monitor, the inside of the bin, the gap behind the
  printer. They are things that really are dark, drawn at a value that shows it
  — not a filter, not a wash, and not more texture. Revision 02 ran 80–232 with
  a mean of 208, so almost everything sat in the top quarter of the range and
  the room read as a light drawing with texture on it.

LINE WEIGHT
  Heavier pen on what is close, lighter on what is far, across a 3.5x range.
  Revisions 01 and 02 both varied it across 1.7x, which reads as uniform. This
  alone is most of what makes the depth legible.

COMPOSITION
  One object sits clearly in the FOREGROUND, cropped by the frame edge, in every
  angle. The crop is the depth cue — a frame edge can only cut what is close —
  and it is what stops the set reading as props laid along one horizontal line.
  The back wall is the lightest plane in the room and stays out of the way.

TONE
  The ground stays the ground. The surface colour is what everything sits on,
  and nothing goes over the top of it globally — if a viewer cannot see the
  paper colour in the frame, the treatment is wrong. There is no wash, no tint
  and no global colour layer anywhere in this family.

  Light is VALUE FALLOFF, not a colour layer. A lamp does not tint a room; it
  makes things near it lighter and things far from it darker, and the falloff
  follows the SHAPES of the objects rather than sitting behind them in a
  rectangle. So the only thing a declared source does here is change how much
  hatch a surface carries: near the lamp, less hatch and more bare ground; two
  metres away, more. Every plate declares its source positions in meta.light so
  a renderer can see where the falloff came from.

  THE INK LINE IS ALWAYS THE DARKEST THING IN FRAME. That is what makes a
  drawing read. Everything else is lighter than it — including the shadows,
  with exactly one exception: contact shadows, which are small and dark, the
  size of the object's footprint rather than a halo around it, and which may go
  darker than the line.

  HATCH SELECTIVELY. Furniture that needs weight — the desk apron and legs, the
  chair, the printer, the bin, the monitor shell — carries a neutral ink hatch,
  capped well below the line's value. The wall, the floor, the paper props and
  the desk surface are left as ground. Texture is only depth when some things
  have it and some do not; hatching everything at one density cancels itself
  out.

  Colour in the room is material only — a terracotta pot, a dying plant, post-it
  yellow. The plate palette's data roles (down, up, attention, other-party) do
  not appear here, and nothing red goes anywhere it could be read as a loss. Two
  earlier passes broke this: a mug ring drawn in `down`, and a sparkline on a
  background monitor drawn in `down` too. Both are now ink.

  WHAT NOT TO ADD, learned the expensive way:
    * No global wash. Revision 01 put a flat night tone under the whole frame
      and rebuilt the room on top of it. Once the paper is gone, every value in
      the drawing has to be found again against a tone that should not be there,
      and the line stops being the darkest thing in frame.
    * No glow pools. A soft radial warm or cold pool behind a prop is not light;
      it is coloured paper taped to the wall. A lamp shows up by taking hatch
      OFF nearby surfaces.
    * No dark window. A solid black rectangle becomes the subject and pulls the
      eye off the host every time. The window is closed blinds in the wood
      colour, with the night showing in a few of the gaps between slats.
    * No orange doorway. A lit opening is drawn by LEAVING THE PAPER ALONE and
      hatching only the top of the opening, where the hall light does not reach.

DENNIS OUTRANKS THE ROOM
  He is the highest-contrast object in any frame he is in, because he is the
  reason anyone is watching. Nothing in the set is allowed to reach his value,
  and he is NOT lit to match the room — an earlier revision tinted him from the
  room's sources and that is precisely what made him the same value as the desk
  behind him.

  The test, and it outranks everything above: composite a host cut-out onto a
  room plate and look at the two together. If your eye does not go to him
  first, the room is too loud.

SLOTS
  All angles:
    title        section title, upper area. Optional — most beats leave it
                 empty and carry the title on a paper/headline-band instead.
    host-anchor  where the host plate's figure goes. Not a point: an area,
                 sized for the 9:16 host at the depth this angle implies.

                 The region's HEIGHT is the host's target height. Scale the
                 host plate until its `figure` slot height equals this
                 height, then sit the host's floorLineY on this region's
                 bottom edge (which is this plate's floorLineY). The width is
                 advisory — it says how much lateral room he has — and is
                 never used to scale him. meta.hostAnchor repeats this
                 contract per plate, so the renderer never has to guess how
                 big Dennis is.
  desk-corner adds:
    caption      names an object in the set. Lower area, small.
  wall-of-calls (22):
    kicker                      one line above the wall
    ticker-N / date-N / outcome-N   seven rows, top to bottom
                                ticker = the symbol; date = when the call was
                                made; outcome = what happened. outcome takes
                                up or down, never neutral — a call that went
                                nowhere does not go on this wall.

SEQUENCE
  1  room plate up on the cut
  2  title, if this beat carries one
  3  host in, on host-anchor
  4  data plate over the room, if any
  For wall-of-calls: kicker, then rows top to bottom, one row per beat. Seven
  rows is seven beats — do not reveal the wall at once, the whole point is
  the accumulation.

TRAPS
  * Do not crop a 16:9 room to get 9:16. The 9:16 plate is re-authored: the
    far wall, the desk depth and the host-anchor are all different.
  * wide and wide-tight are not interchangeable at the same beat — the eyeline
    changes. Cut between them only across a beat boundary.
  * The screens in the set are set dressing and carry no slots. Anything
    readable goes on a data plate composited over the room, not on the monitor.
    The monitor panel is a source, so it is left as BARE GROUND inside its
    bezel — the lightest thing in frame. Revision 01 hatched it cold blue,
    which made the brightest object in the room a mid-tone.
  * Do not add a third light. Two sources are what make the depth legible; a
    third flattens the room back out and there is nothing in the set at this
    hour that would cast one.
  * Props overlap on purpose — a mug half behind a monitor, papers over the
    keyboard, a cable crossing the desk edge. Do not "tidy" an angle by
    separating them into a row; the overlap is the depth.
